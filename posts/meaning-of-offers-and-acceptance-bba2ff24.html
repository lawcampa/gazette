Meaning of offers and acceptance

By Justina C. · 6 November 2026 · 9 min read

law

CARLILL v. CARBOLIC SMOKE BALL COMPANY (1893) 1 QB 256

Area of Law: Law of Contract Court: Court of Appeal, England Date: 7 December 1893 Citation: [1893] 1 QB 256 Judges: Lindley LJ, Bowen LJ and A. L. Smith LJ.


1. Introduction

The case of Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Company is one of the leading authorities in the Law of Contract, particularly on the principles of offer and acceptance, unilateral contracts, consideration and intention to create legal relations.

This established that acceptance of such an offer may be made through the performance of the conditions stipulated in the offer without necessarily communicating acceptance to the offeror. This means that acceptable could be by expression or by conduct, or by performance


2. Facts of the Case

The defendants, Carbolic Smoke Ball Company, manufactured and sold a product known as the Carbolic Smoke Ball, which they advertised as a preventive measure against influenza and other related illnesses.


On 13 November 1891, the company published an advertisement in the Pall Mall Gazette and other newspapers, promising to pay £100 to any person who contracted influenza after using the smoke ball three times daily for two weeks according to the printed instructions.


The advertisement further stated that the company had deposited £1,000 with the Alliance Bank, Regent Street, as evidence of its sincerity and intention to fulfil the promise.


Mrs Louisa Elizabeth Carlill saw the advertisement and, relying on the company's representation, purchased a smoke ball.

She used the product three times daily for the prescribed period of two weeks, following the instructions provided by the company.


However, despite complying with the instructions, she contracted influenza.

Consequently, Mrs Carlill claimed the promised £100 reward from the company. The company refused to pay, arguing that the advertisement was not a legally binding offer.

Mrs Carlill subsequently instituted an action against the company for breach of contract.


The trial court decided in favour of Mrs Carlill. Dissatisfied with the decision, the company appealed to the Court of Appeal.

3. Issues for Determination

The principal issue before the Court of Appeal was:

Whether the advertisement published by the Carbolic Smoke Ball Company constituted a valid and legally binding offer capable of acceptance by Mrs Carlill.

Other subsidiary issues included:

1. Whether an advertisement addressed to the general public could constitute a valid offer.

2. Whether Mrs Carlill communicated her acceptance to the company.

3. Whether there was consideration to support the company's promise.

4. Whether the company demonstrated an intention to create legal relations.

5. Whether the advertisement was merely a promotional statement (puff) rather than a serious contractual offer.

4. Arguments of the Parties

A. Arguments of the Defendant (Carbolic Smoke Ball Company)

The company argued that:

1. The advertisement was merely an invitation to treat and not a legally binding offer.

2. The advertisement was directed at the general public, and it was not possible to make a contractual offer to the entire world.

3. Mrs Carlill did not communicate her acceptance to the company before using the smoke ball.

4. There was no consideration moving from Mrs Carlill to the company to support the alleged agreement.

5. The advertisement was merely a promotional statement intended to attract customers and not a serious promise capable of creating contractual obligations.

B. Arguments of the Plaintiff (Mrs Carlill)

Mrs Carlill maintained that:

1. The advertisement contained a clear and definite promise to pay £100 to anyone who fulfilled the stated conditions.

2. She accepted the offer by purchasing and using the smoke ball in accordance with the company's instructions.

3. The company's promise that £1,000 had been deposited in the bank demonstrated its intention to be legally bound.

4. Her purchase and use of the smoke ball constituted sufficient consideration for the company's promise.

Consequently, she argued that a valid contract existed between herself and the company.

5. Decision of the Court

The Court of Appeal unanimously dismissed the company's appeal and held in favour of Mrs Carlill.

The court decided that the advertisement constituted a valid unilateral offer made to the general public.

Mrs Carlill accepted the offer by performing the conditions stipulated in the advertisement.

The court further held that:

The advertisement was a genuine offer and not merely an invitation to treat.

Communication of acceptance was unnecessary because the offer contemplated acceptance through performance.

There was sufficient consideration to support the company's promise.

The deposit of £1,000 in the bank demonstrated the company's intention to create legal relations.

  1. The company was therefore legally obligated to pay Mrs Carlill the promised £100.
  2. 6. Legal Principles Established (Ratio Decidendi)
  3. The case established several important principles of contract law.
  4. A. An Advertisement Can Constitute a Valid Offer

Generally, advertisements are regarded as invitations to treat rather than offers.

An invitation to treat merely invites members of the public to make offers, whereas an offer expresses a willingness to enter into a contract upon acceptance.


However, the court established that an advertisement may constitute a valid offer where its language demonstrates a clear intention to be legally bound upon the fulfilment of specified conditions.


In this case, the company's promise to pay £100 to anyone who contracted influenza after using the smoke ball as directed was sufficiently definite.


Legal principle: An advertisement may constitute a valid offer where it contains a clear and definite promise demonstrating an intention to be legally bound.

B. Unilateral Contract

The case is a leading authority on unilateral contracts.

A unilateral contract is an agreement in which one party makes a promise in exchange for the performance of a specified act by another party.


In this case, the company promised to pay £100 to anyone who fulfilled the conditions stated in the advertisement and subsequently contracted influenza.

Mrs Carlill was not required to make a separate promise to use the smoke ball. Her performance of the stipulated conditions constituted acceptance.

Legal principle: A unilateral offer may be accepted through the performance of the conditions specified by the offeror.

C. Acceptance by Conduct or Performance

Ordinarily, acceptance must be communicated to the offeror.

However, the court recognised that where an offer invites acceptance through the performance of a particular act, the performance itself may constitute sufficient acceptance.


Mrs Carlill was therefore not required to notify the company before using the smoke ball.

Her conduct in using the product according to the stipulated instructions amounted to acceptance of the offer.

Legal principle: Where an offer contemplates acceptance by performance, communication of acceptance may be unnecessary.


D. Consideration

Consideration is something of legal value given in exchange for a promise.

The company argued that Mrs Carlill had provided no consideration for its promise to pay £100.

However, the court rejected this argument.

The purchase and use of the smoke ball constituted sufficient consideration. Mrs Carlill incurred inconvenience by using the product three times daily for two weeks.

Furthermore, the company derived a commercial benefit from the increased sale of its products resulting from the advertisement.

Legal principle: An act performed at the request of the promisor, which confers a benefit on the promisor or constitutes a detriment to the promisee, may amount to sufficient consideration.

E. Intention to Create Legal Relations

For a contract to be legally enforceable, the parties must demonstrate an intention to create legal relations.

The court held that the company's advertisement demonstrated such intention.

In particular, the statement that £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank to demonstrate the company's sincerity was strong evidence that the promise was intended to be taken seriously.

The company could not therefore successfully argue that its advertisement was merely promotional language.

Legal principle: The intention to create legal relations may be inferred from the words and conduct of the parties, including the circumstances surrounding the transaction.

7. Obiter Dicta

An important observation in the judgment was the explanation that an offer made to the general public does not necessarily create a contract with everybody.

Rather, a contract arises between the offeror and any individual who performs the conditions specified in the offer.

This explains how an offer can be addressed to an indefinite number of people while producing contractual obligations only when an individual accepts it.

8. Significance of the Case in Contract Law

The decision in Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Company remains significant for the following reasons:

1. Offer: It established that an advertisement could constitute a valid offer rather than merely an invitation to treat.

2. Acceptance: It recognised acceptance through conduct or performance without prior notification where the offer contemplates such acceptance.

3. Unilateral contracts: It provided an authoritative illustration of how unilateral contracts operate.

4. Consideration: It demonstrated that inconvenience or detriment incurred by the promisee could constitute sufficient consideration.

5. Intention to create legal relations: It demonstrated that the seriousness of a contractual promise could be inferred from the circumstances, including the deposit of money in a bank.

6. General offer: It established that an offer could be made to the public at large and accepted by anyone who fulfils its conditions.

9. Conclusion

The case of Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Company illustrates the fundamental principles governing the formation of a valid contract.

The Court of Appeal held that the company's advertisement constituted a unilateral offer to the general public, which Mrs Carlill accepted by performing the stipulated conditions.

The court further established that communication of acceptance was unnecessary in the circumstances, that sufficient consideration existed and that the company demonstrated an intention to create legal relations.

Consequently, the company was held liable for its promise and required to pay Mrs Carlill the £100 reward.

The central lesson from the case is that an advertisement containing a clear and definite promise may create a legally binding contract where a person accepts it by performing the conditions stipulated in the advertisement.
