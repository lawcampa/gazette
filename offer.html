Offer and Acceptance: How a Contract Begins

By Law Companion contributor

Every contract starts with an agreement, and in law an agreement is found by looking for two things: an offer and an acceptance. If either is missing, there is no contract, however friendly the discussions between the parties may have been.

What is an offer?

An offer is a clear statement by one person (the offeror) that they are willing to be bound on certain terms as soon as the other person (the offeree) agrees. It must be definite and show an intention to be bound.

An offer is different from an invitation to treat. Displaying goods in a shop window, advertising a sale or asking for tenders are usually invitations to treat. They invite others to make an offer and do not bind the person who made them. A leading illustration is Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (1953), where goods on a shelf were held to be an invitation to treat.

What is acceptance?

Acceptance is an unqualified agreement to the terms of the offer. Three points matter most:

Acceptance must match the offer exactly, must be communicated to the offeror, and must be made while the offer is still open.

If the offeree changes the terms, that is a counter-offer. It ends the original offer, and the parties are back to negotiating. Hyde v Wrench (1840) is the standard case on this.

When does the offer end?

An offer can come to an end by revocation before it is accepted, by rejection, by lapse of time or by the death of a party in some situations. Once it has ended, it can no longer be accepted.

Quick summary

  • A contract needs an offer and an acceptance.
  • An invitation to treat is not an offer.
  • Acceptance must be unqualified and communicated.
  • A counter-offer ends the original offer.
